Robert Abel & Associates had a surprisingly coherent style despite moving from optical effects in the 1970s to vector and raster CGI in the 1980s. The technology changed, but the underlying aesthetic remained fairly constant.

The best shorthand I found is:

**luminous graphic design + cinematic motion + technological spectacle + pop glamour.**

Their work rarely tried to make the technology disappear. Instead, it made the *newness of the image* part of the attraction.

### 1. Light was treated as a graphic material

The most immediately recognizable Abel look is intensely luminous imagery: saturated colors against dark backgrounds, glowing contours, streaks, halos, radiant edges and objects that appear to be made partly from light.

A major early technique was what designer Richard Taylor called **“Candy-Apple Neon.”** In the 7UP *Bubbles* work, live action, rotoscoping, hand animation and optical processes produced chrome-like glowing edges around figures, symbols and typography. The People's Graphic Design Archive describes the spot as a psychedelic transformation of 7UP imagery into chorus lines, butterflies and luminous graphics, and identifies this glowing treatment as something the studio became widely known for. ([People’s Graphic Design Archive][1])

This wasn't simply a 1970s psychedelic accident. Taylor had previously designed rock light shows, and historian Tom Sito specifically attributes RA&A's signature “hot, glowing colors” to him. ([Moving Image Source][2])

So visually:

**deep black → saturated object → bright core → soft bloom → luminous trail**

is almost an Abel grammar.

### 2. Everything wants to move through depth

RA&A's imagery rarely feels like flat motion graphics even when the source material is essentially graphic design. Things **fly toward you, rotate, tunnel away, sweep past the camera or leave trails in perspective**.

That comes directly from their production methods. Abel and Con Pederson adapted computerized motion-control techniques descended from *2001: A Space Odyssey*. Their facility eventually contained multiple motion-control tracks and boom systems alongside optical printers, projection systems and graphics hardware. ([Ohio State Pressbooks][3])

Slit-scan and streak photography reinforced the effect. Instead of merely moving a graphic across a frame, movement could stretch its light through space and exposure time. The Ohio State history describes the resulting imagery as a “psychedelic flow of colors.” ([Ohio State Pressbooks][3])

The consequence is important: **Abel motion is usually spatial rather than merely positional.**

A logo doesn't slide in.
It *arrives from another dimension.*

### 3. Graphic design remained underneath the spectacle

One of the easiest mistakes when imitating Abel is to focus entirely on bloom, chrome and vintage CGI.

Robert Abel was fundamentally a **designer-filmmaker**. Before RA&A, he worked around Saul Bass and John Whitney; UCLA also identifies Charles and Ray Eames, Bass and Whitney as formative influences. His 1963 *Freight Yard Symphony* already used bold modernist graphics, photo collage, rhythmic camera movement and music decades before the famous CG material. UCLA compares its visual thinking to Mondrian, Oskar Fischinger, Saul Bass and the Eameses. ([UCLA Film & Television Archive][4])

That lineage explains why an Abel composition tends to contain:

**simple silhouettes, strong geometry, isolated symbols, carefully controlled negative space, iconic rather than cluttered imagery, and extremely legible transformations.**

The effects are elaborate, but the *idea you are looking at* is usually simple.

That is a major distinction between authentic Abel-style imagery and generic “1980s CGI.”

### 4. Transformation is more important than cutting

Objects in their work frequently undergo a chain of visual states.

A figure becomes an outline.
An outline becomes a streak.
A streak becomes geometry.
Geometry opens into space.
A product becomes an abstract emblem.

The *Bubbles* commercial is especially characteristic: the imagery morphs between a recognizable 7UP figure, repeated dancers and an abstract neon butterfly. ([People’s Graphic Design Archive][1])

This gives many RA&A films a **continuous metamorphic quality**. Instead of montage being created exclusively through edits, one visual event generates the next.

It makes thirty seconds feel like a miniature audiovisual journey.

### 5. The artificiality is seductive, not sterile

This became even clearer once the studio moved into raster CGI.

Early computer graphics were often associated with CAD, engineering diagrams and visibly mathematical wireframes. Abel actually worried that clients would associate “computer graphics” with CAD/CAM because it looked, in his words as recalled by Sito, “cold and phony.” The studio therefore presented the computer as simply another animation technique. ([Moving Image Source][2])

Their solution was basically to make synthetic imagery **sensual**.

Hence:

* polished metal
* glass
* reflections
* glowing translucent surfaces
* smooth curves
* rich saturated color
* graceful motion
* dramatic darkness
* glamorous human forms

The famous **Brilliance / “Sexy Robot”** commercial is almost a manifesto for this idea. Its chrome female figure combined unusually fluid human motion with highly reflective synthetic skin. Contemporary recollections emphasize how astonishing the combination of believable movement and chrome rendering appeared at the time. ([Moving Image Source][2])

So the Abel future isn't utilitarian.

It's **luxurious, theatrical and slightly erotic**.

### 6. Chrome was important because it made the environment visible

The “chrome 80s CGI” cliché partly obscures what makes Abel's use of reflection interesting.

A mirror surface has almost no visual identity of its own. It becomes interesting through **everything around it**.

That lets a very simple digital object acquire extraordinary visual complexity without requiring detailed texture. In *Brilliance*, the reflective character continuously reorganizes the scene into moving highlights.

Conceptually this fits Abel perfectly:

**form + movement + light = image**

rather than form + elaborate surface decoration.

The result is imagery that is rich but often structurally minimal.

### 7. The hybrid look matters

Another defining trait is that their work doesn't divide neatly into “analog Abel” and “CG Abel.”

Their aesthetic arose from **photo-fusion**: motion-control photography, live action, backlit artwork, rotoscoping, animation, multiple exposures, optical compositing and eventually computer imagery all combined in a single shot. The Visual Effects Society's retrospective calls these painstaking combinations RA&A's “stock in trade.” ([Animation World Network][5])

Even their early vector CGI was frequently photographed from an Evans & Sutherland display through colored filters and multiple exposures rather than simply output as a clean computer image. ([Communication Arts][6])

That explains an important texture of their work:

**it is digital geometry photographed like light.**

There is often softness, blooming, film grain, exposure buildup and optical contamination around otherwise mathematically precise forms.

That's much closer to the actual Abel look than rendering crisp retro wireframes.

### 8. Precision and excess coexist

There is an interesting tension throughout the work.

Composition and camera movement are **extremely controlled** because motion control demands repeatability.

But the image produced by that precision can be completely excessive:

glowing trails, dozens of exposures, impossible rotations, rainbow color, infinite space, mirrored figures.

So you get:

> **engineering precision in the service of visual delirium.**

That may be the single most useful conceptual description of the studio.

RA&A itself institutionalized this combination. Abel said one of the studio's distinguishing qualities was pooling talents, and the company deliberately paired art directors with technical directors. ([Animation World Network][5]) Ohio State's history similarly argues that their particular strength was bringing traditional effects, cinematography and filmmaking knowledge into CGI rather than treating computer graphics as an isolated discipline. ([Ohio State Pressbooks][3])

### 9. The imagery promises revelation

There is also a recurring dramatic structure to Abel commercials.

You start with something only partially understood.

Then:

**approach → reveal → transformation → escalation → logo/product resolution.**

The camera often seems to be *discovering* an impossible object.

This relates to Abel's own conception of effects as magic. A longtime collaborator recalled that Abel thought of visual effects in Méliès-like terms: tricks should serve an experience rather than exist only for their own sake. ([Animation World Network][5])

His criterion for a successful commercial was wonderfully revealing: it should somehow evoke the sensation of something familiar while nevertheless being something you had never seen. ([Animation World Network][5])

That is exactly what the imagery does.

Chrome woman: human / impossible.
Butterfly: recognizable / made of neon.
Landscape: spatial / obviously synthetic.
Logo: familiar / revealed cosmically.

## Two distinct Abel periods

It's useful not to collapse everything into one “retro CGI” aesthetic.

**1970s Abel** is more psychedelic and photographic: slit-scan, streaks, backlighting, repeated figures, intense primary color, optical layering, live action transformed into graphic abstraction. *7UP Bubbles* is probably the clearest specimen. UCLA calls it kaleidoscopic, and the archive regards the highly stylized 7UP campaigns as emblematic of RA&A's unique aesthetic. ([UCLA Film & Television Archive][7])

**1980s Abel** becomes more synthetic and spatial: luminous vectors, impossible geometrical landscapes, reflective 3D objects, smooth spline surfaces, flying cameras and digital characters. Representative pieces include the Panasonic *Glider*, TRW work, *High Fidelity*, *Brilliance*, Benson & Hedges spots and their material for *Tron*. Ohio State documents that progression from E&S vector imagery into the studio's raster system. ([Ohio State Pressbooks][3])

But both periods share **light, transformation, depth, spectacle and graphic clarity**.

## If I were making an “Abel style sheet”

I'd reduce it to one vocabulary:

**Palette:** black, electric blue, cyan, magenta, saturated red, spectral rainbow, white-hot highlights.

**Materials:** chrome, glass, neon, polished plastic, emissive linework, translucent light.

**Lighting:** hard rim lights plus enormous optical bloom; objects frequently read first as luminous contours.

**Composition:** one dominant object or motif; deep negative space; centered or strongly perspectival arrangements.

**Camera:** long deliberate moves, push-ins, orbital movement, dramatic perspective changes.

**Motion:** smooth, graceful, mathematically precise; repetition and trails turn motion into pattern.

**Transitions:** morph, dissolve, streak, explode, pass through, transform—avoid ordinary cuts when an image can generate the next image.

**Texture:** mix geometric precision with photographic imperfection: diffusion, glow, grain, overexposure, color bleed.

**Subject matter:** technology portrayed as magic, futurity portrayed as glamour.

**Direction:** continuously reveal something; don't merely display it.

And I'd put one rule above all the others:

### **Don't make “retro CGI.” Make an impossible graphic idea feel like it has been photographed.**

That gets much closer to what Robert Abel & Associates were actually doing.

For the demoscene angle from your previous question, this is also why Abel is such a productive reference: their best work turns a **technical limitation or breakthrough into the visual language itself**, but then subjects it to rigorous graphic design and direction. The effect is never merely “look what the machine can do”; the machine's peculiar capabilities become the aesthetic. ([Communication Arts][6])
